# Data engine validation

What was checked about the data engine (Andrew), how, and what it means. This is the input for the
team's validation work (Member 6); it covers the engine's science, not the viability rules or UI.

- **Reproduce:** `cd backend` then `.venv\Scripts\python.exe scripts\validate_engine.py` (a few minutes;
  downloads ~25 MB of JPL kernels and LROC data on first run, cached in `data/cache/` and `data/raw/`).
- **Stored report:** `data/validation/engine_validation.json`: per-site results, the `run` it came from (date, code
  commit, site data version and range) and a `summary` (`backend/app/engine/validation_summary.py`), also served
  at `/api/validation`. **The results section below is generated from that report** by the same script
  (`--doc-only` regenerates it without re-running); a test fails if the two disagree. Don't edit the numbers by hand.
- **Data checked:** all 24 curated site files `data/sites/*.json` (8 Artemis III candidate-region sites, the
  Shackleton control, 15 past landings).

The results are of different kinds and say different things. None of them is a formal uncertainty or
confidence interval: they are agreement measures, each with its own scope.

| Kind | What it shows | What it doesn't |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Software agreement (ephemeris) | Our Sun/Earth directions match an independent computation | That either matches the real sky better than the ephemerides do (both are JPL ephemerides) |
| 2. Method agreement (horizon) | Our horizon geometry matches an independent geometry on the same elevation data | Errors in the elevation data itself |
| 3. Comparison with observations (LROC) | Modelled sunlight vs orbital images of the real surface | Mission dates (the images are from 2010-11), single moments (yearly totals), sub-pixel detail |
| 4. Software tests | The code does what it is specified to do, case by case | Anything about real-world accuracy |

## 1. Software agreement: Sun and Earth positions vs an independent ephemeris

- **Ours:** JPL Horizons API, DE441, apparent azimuth/elevation from a `coord@301` site (Mean-Earth frame).
- **Reference:** Skyfield 1.55 with JPL DE421 and the Moon's Mean-Earth frame `MOON_ME_DE421`
  (`moon_080317.tf`, `moon_pa_de421_1900-2050.bpc`), apparent positions (light-time and aberration).
  Different software and a different ephemeris; only the frame definition is shared.
- **Verdicts:** every sampled hour is also run through our horizon with the reference positions; a "change"
  is a Sun or Earth visible/blocked verdict that flips.
- **Reading it:** the differences are about the same at every site because they come from our file rounding
  (angles are stored to 0.001°, so up to 0.0005° per axis, about 0.0007° combined); the two ephemerides differ
  by less. This also confirms our parsing, time handling (UTC), azimuth convention and frame choice end to end.

## 2. Method agreement: horizon geometry vs an independent method

Since generator 1.2 the engine walks true great circles from the site and computes elevation angles from 3D
Moon-fixed vectors (`compute_horizon_latlon` in `app/engine/horizon.py`), so it works anywhere on the Moon. The
check recomputes the horizon with the engine's **original** method: straight lines in the south-polar map plane
and a central-angle formula (`compute_horizon`), fed the **same** elevation data at the same spacing
(`_SameDataPlane` in the script). That isolates the geometry: the two share no geometric code. All 720
directions (every 0.5°) are compared. Bearings follow Horizons' azimuth convention ("North(0) → East(90) →
South(180) → West(270)"); a horizon mirrored east↔west would disagree by degrees, so the check also confirms
the convention.

The differences grow with distance from the pole (largest at Nobile Rim 1 and Chandrayaan-3), where the flat-map
method's straight-line approximation is weakest; the great-circle method has no such approximation. Sites north
of 60°S can't use the polar map plane, so this check doesn't apply to them; they rely on the same great-circle
code, plus unit tests at equatorial positions (bare sphere, ridge in a known direction).

## 3. Comparison with observations: sunlight vs LROC orbital images

- **Reference:** LROC `WAC_POLE_ILL_TWI_SOUTH_100M` (Speyerer & Robinson 2013, Icarus 222, 122–136): the
  percentage of time each 100 m pixel was seen lit in ~3,800 Wide-Angle Camera images,
  2010-02-15 to 2011-02-05, covering 88–90°S. This is the downloadable version of Quickmap's illumination layer.
  Its map projection matches ours to 0.1 m.
- **Fair comparison:** the model is re-run for **the same period** with the sensor **at the surface** (LROC sees
  the ground; our site files use 2 m). Sites outside 88–90°S (Malapert, Haworth, Nobile, Mons Mouton,
  Faustini and the others) can't be compared.

**Reading it:**
- **Area averages:** over the grid around each covered site the model is not biased high or low by more than a
  few percentage points, and the patterns correlate.
- **Single points on a light/dark edge can disagree a lot.** Connecting Ridge's own LROC pixel says 8.7% while a
  neighbour 100 m away says 30.1%; the model's point value is far from that pixel. A point model and a 100 m
  camera pixel can't agree on such an edge. Likely causes: WAC geolocation (tens of metres), the camera's
  conservative "lit" threshold (grazing light on slopes can read as dark), and pixel averaging.
- **What to do with that:** treat a single site's sunlight % near a light/dark edge as indicative, and prefer area
  or margin-based reasoning there. The differences in the table are observed discrepancies at a few sites, not
  an error bar that applies everywhere.
- **Yearly totals can't test the azimuth convention.** Our horizon, a mirrored one and a 180°-rotated one score
  almost the same (in the stored report: `spatial.mirrored_EW`, `spatial.rotated_180`), because over a year the
  Sun passes every azimuth and the pattern around the pole is mirror-symmetric. That's why check 2 exists.

## 4. Software tests

Run `cd backend; .venv\Scripts\python.exe -m pytest` and `cd frontend; npm test` for the current counts (they
change with every commit, so they aren't quoted here). The engine-related ones:
- `backend/tests/test_engine.py`, `test_engine_side_by_side.py`, `test_timeline.py`, `test_ephemeris_chunks.py`:
  horizon geometry (bare sphere, ridge in a known direction), visibility and the Sun-disk fraction, Horizons

- `frontend/src/viz/geometry.test.ts`: placing objects at the engine's azimuth/elevation in the 3D view.

## Earlier runs (superseded)

Kept so older copies of these numbers can be recognised; **don't quote them**.
- **Generator 1.1 (4 sites, 2 years):** max position difference 0.0007°, and **1 of 23,400** sampled verdicts
  changed (Connecting Ridge, 2027-06-29 18:00 UTC, where the Sun's top edge cleared the skyline by 0.00002° in
  the reference and sat just below it in ours). Superseded by the generator 1.2 run above (all 24 sites,
  2026–2030), which found no changed verdict. The horizon check then used 36 directions and a 3D-vector
  method; it now compares all 720 directions against the original flat-map method.

### What changed from generator 1.1
The engine now samples a whole-Moon elevation registry with great circles (so it works anywhere). For the original
sites the horizons moved by up to 0.2–0.4° (Connecting Ridge, Peak Near Shackleton), and at **Malapert** by up to
1.6°: Malapert lies outside the 5 m product (87.5°S), and its near field now comes from the finer **20 m** product
instead of the 30 m one. With 20 m detail, the flat summit top within ~100 m of a 2 m mast blocks a very low Sun,
so Malapert's sunlight went from **82.6% to 77.1%** (Earth: 99.9% to 100%). The finer data is the better source.
Other summaries changed by ≤ 0.3 points.

## Physical sanity checks on past landings

From the generator 1.2 site files (`data/sites/*.json`, their `summary`), not from the stored report: all 6
Apollo sites, SLIM, Blue Ghost, Chang'e 3/5, Chandrayaan-3 and IM-1 have Earth in view 100% of the time;
Chang'e 4 and 6 (far side) never (33–52° below the horizon); the Sun is up 47–50% of the time at low latitudes,
as expected.

## Assumptions and limitations

| Assumption | Effect | Size / status |
|---|---|---|
| No atmosphere: geometric line of sight, no refraction | None; the Moon has no atmosphere | Exact |
| Frame: everything in Mean-Earth (ME) | Checked: Horizons header and LOLA docs | Guarded in code |
| Earth's libration (its wobble in the lunar sky) | Included: Horizons gives the apparent direction from the site in the Moon-fixed frame, so the Moon's physical and optical libration move Earth about ±7–8° around its mean position each month (in our data its elevation swings 13.5° at Connecting Ridge, −7.4° to +6.1°, and 15.2° at Apollo 11). This is why Earth rises and sets at the south pole | Exact (part of the ephemeris) |
| Terrain: LOLA via Moon Trek at 5 m (to 5 km), 30 m (to 45 km), 200 m resampled (to ~294 km) | Small features below the resolution are smoothed | Coarser tiers matter most at grazing angles |
| Terrain beyond ~294 km ignored | The Moon's curvature drops it ≥ 25 km below the eye line | Negligible |
| Horizon every 0.5° of azimuth, linear between | Narrow notches or peaks can be missed | ≤ 0.25° in azimuth |
| Horizon rays along great circles (since 1.2; earlier versions used straight lines on the polar map) | The old method agrees to within the per-site values in the results (check 2) | Exact geometry |
| One sensor height, 2 m above ground | Real panels and antennas sit higher or lower | Use the same height everywhere until a mission config needs more |
| Solar disk vs a flat skyline across its 0.53° width | Disk fraction slightly off when the skyline is steep under the Sun | Small; only affects partial-Sun hours |
| Earth = its centre point (visible if the centre clears the terrain) | Earth is ~1.9° wide; partly visible Earth counts as blocked or visible by its centre | Margin column lets users add their own buffer |
| Horizons places the site on the 1737.4 km sphere, not at its true height | Parallax | < 0.001° |
| Hourly samples | Rise/set times known to ±30 min | Fine for planning; finer steps on request |
| Data covers 2026-01-01 to 2031-01-01 (extended from 2027–2028; every hour of the old range is unchanged) | Dates outside this range have no real data | Other dates need a re-run of `precompute.py` |
| Unknown terrain (custom sites only) is `null`, never guessed | Some hours can be "unknown" | Curated sites: 0% unknown |
| 3D view: by default terrain casts shadows only within ~6 km of the lander (one shadow map; none in the Low graphics setting). The "Far shadows" option (cascaded shadow maps) extends them to everything on screen | Without it, distant ground can look lit when it is in shadow. With it, only terrain that is drawn casts shadows, so a mountain off screen or behind the camera can't shadow the view | Display only: the numbers, the lander's lighting and the skyline use the engine's full 300 km horizon |
| Sunlight % at one point | Sharp light/dark edges make single points sensitive to tens of metres | See check 3: indicative only near edges; no formal error bar |

## Suggested follow-ups (for the validation member)

- **Time-resolved illumination:** LROC's sub-solar-longitude mosaics (`NAC_POLE_SOUTH_CM_*`) or the WAC
  illumination movie would test individual lit/dark moments, which yearly totals can't.
- **Earth visibility reference:** no observed product exists; a cross-check against another horizon model
  (e.g. PGDA's LOLA-based polar illumination and Earth-visibility products) would be the next step.
- **Sensitivity to sensor height:** re-run with 0, 2 and 10 m to show how much a mast helps at each site.
